Where Do Sine Waves Come From?

Trigonometry

"I never really got why sine looked like a wave..."

Here's the secret: take a point going around a circle at constant speed. Now just track its height over time. That's it. That's the sine wave.

The "Aha!" moment

Circular motion projected onto a line creates simple harmonic motion. This is why springs, pendulums, and sound waves all follow sine curves.

# The connection is this simple: y = radius * sin(angular_velocity * time)

When Waves Meet

Superposition

"What happens when two waves hit each other?"

They add up. It's that simple. When peaks align, you get a bigger wave (constructive). When a peak meets a trough, they cancel out (destructive). This is how noise-canceling headphones work!

Why this matters

Interference explains everything from guitar harmonics to why your WiFi has dead spots to how quantum computers work.

Why Does i² = -1?

Complex Numbers

"i squared equals negative one always seemed like a weird rule someone made up..."

But watch this: multiplying by i is just rotating by 90°. Do it twice? That's 180°. And rotating 180° on the number line takes you from 1 to -1. That's why i × i = -1. It's not a rule—it's geometry!

Mind = Blown

Complex numbers aren't imaginary nonsense. They're the natural language for anything that rotates: electricity, quantum mechanics, signal processing.

Building Shapes from Waves

Fourier

"How can smooth sine waves make a sharp square wave?"

Stack enough of them! A square wave is just sin(x) + sin(3x)/3 + sin(5x)/5 + ... Keep adding odd harmonics and watch the corners get sharper. This is how MP3s, JPEGs, and MRIs actually work.

Fourier's gift to humanity

ANY repeating pattern can be built from sine waves. This single idea powers most of modern signal processing.

# The formula is beautiful: square_wave = sum(4/(n*pi) * sin(n*x) for n in [1,3,5,7,...])

Fields That Flow

Vector Fields

"What exactly IS a vector field?"

Imagine little arrows at every point in space, showing which way things flow. Drop a leaf in a stream and watch where it goes—that's the field in action. This one creates rotation: at every point, the arrow points perpendicular to the radius.

Where you'll see this

Weather maps, magnetic fields, fluid dynamics, game physics. Vector fields are everywhere once you know what to look for.

The Pendulum's Secret

Harmonic Motion

"A swinging pendulum draws a sine wave? Really?"

Plot the angle over time and there it is—a perfect sine curve. The pendulum doesn't know trigonometry; it's just following Newton's laws. But those laws produce the same math as circular motion. Nature rhymes.

Simple harmonic motion

Pendulums, springs, vibrating molecules, guitar strings—they all follow the same pattern because they all obey the same differential equation.
